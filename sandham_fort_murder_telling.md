# The Sandham Fort Tragedy — A Telling

A tale in eight parts.

## Part the first — A Funeral

It's Sunday, 20th of May, 1860. A thousand people and more — some say as many as *two* thousand — are stood around the centuries old Church of St Mary's, in the Kynges Town of Brading, on the Isle of Wight.

The House of Industry cart approaches. It left Sandown just after one o'clock, and now it's nearly two. Seven boxes — you'd hardly call them coffins — have been piled on the back. One is stained black, the other six are whitewashed. Seven boxes into which seven bodies have been forced by the pauper officials, crushed into coffins too small for their contents, no funeral clothing and no attempt to perform the offices you might normally expect, the lids nailed on with a few common nails.

No family, no friends, no pall bearers. Several men of the Isle of Wight artillery militia take the coffins off the cart, carry them into the Church, and place them on three biers in the centre aisle. Two, by two, by two.

The Church is crammed full, hundreds more stuck outside, and the Service is read, by the vicar, the Rev. Isidore Heath. He would be found guilty of heresy by the Bishop of Winchester the following yer, and deprived of his benefice, but *that* has nothing to do with *this* — *that* is another story for another day, perhaps.

The solemn service concluded, the procession is reformed, and makes it way to the grave, a vast hole, near the grave of *the Young Cottager, Little Jane*, and next to that of Mrs. Berry ("Forgive, blest shade, the tributary tear"). Eight feet wide, and five feet deep (though others say it was barely three), and seven feet long. The remaining rites of the church are performed, and the bodies lowered, as the thousands, gathered round, weep and wail.

All the time, more people are arriving — they thought the ceremony was at four, not two.

The vicar addresses the crowd on the necessity of controlling tempers, and of resisting temptation. A single, condemnatory heckle from the crowd, but the vicar declines to engage with it. This isn't the time for feeding the trolls, this isn't the time for controversy, or debate.

The proceedings are over, and the crowd makes its way down the hill, and up, to the market place, where an independent minister, Mr. Beresford, addresses them from steps of the Old Town Hall. And it is done.

In the days that followed, correspondents to the Isle of Wight Observer debate whether a subscription should be formed to pay for a memorial, and a suggestion is even made that the bodies should be exhumed, then reburied, with proper dignity.

So who were the seven who were buried that day? The tale is, perhaps, the darkest historical tale I know, at least, the darkest *Island* tale, darker even than that of the tale of Michael Morey.

So let's go back, five or six days or so.

## Part the Second — The Fort

At the Borough Sessions, in Newport, on the Monday before, a corporal of the Isle of Wight Artillery Militia, stationed at Sandown Fort, was charged with assaulting Elizabeth Dove, landlady of the White Horse, in Nodehill. As the story tells it, she'd tried to prevent a fight between him and a stranger who'd challenged him to dance. On being fined, the corporal refused to pay, but Captain Manners, the newly appointed Captain of the Fort, who'd arrived on the Island just two or three weeks before, said the regiment would cover the costs of the fine, and the soldier was set free.

But that is not part of the tale, either.

At the time, 1860, Sandown fort was at Sandham Gardens, adjacent to Fort Street now, rather than up at the Wild Heart Sanctuary. *(That fort, the Yaverland battery, didn't start its construction until the following year, 1861; Sandham Fort was then demolished, in 1864.)* An old fort, it was on marshy ground, or maybe, make that *swampy* ground, and in a rather dilapidated state. This was the time before the causeway connecting St Helen's and Bembridge, and the reclamation of the land just in from Brading Haven, a time when Brading Quay was still operational, and the whole area between Brading and Yaverland was still very much marshland, even more than it is today. The fort had previously been up put up for sale "to the Nobility and Gentry" in 1831 as a desirable residence with a unique character and stunning sea views — you can read the advertising blurb then, and compare it to estate agents' blurb now — some things *never* change! — but a sale appears *not* to have gone through.

Sandown itself was a romantic little village, with its craggy undercliff, and summer glories, and was just starting to open up as a holiday destination.

As well as the Island militia, the Coast Brigade of the Royal Artillery were also stationed there. The fort itself was home to just eighteen people — three officers, three officers servants, the master gunner and his family, and the another NCO and his wife — the rest of the soldiers living at the top end of Sandown, in the barracks, where the Heights is today. On the parade ground there, the men would have a regular daily drill.

The fort was approached by a narrow wooden bridge over a small moat, although some might call it a *ditch*, with a gate that opened onto small court yard, thirty-five paces square. There was no other way in, or out. A few humble dwelling-houses to the right and left, for the NCOs and their families, and the officers' quarters facing the gate. 

There wasn't much real need for posting sentries at night, so custom had it that the gates would be locked by one of the officers' servants (Bevis, Chatfield, or Racket) after tattoo roll call at half-nine each night; they'd keep hold of the keys and let back in any of the Officers who'd been out for the night.

Captain Manners, in particular, had quickly got into the habit of heading up to the Star and Garter for his dinner each evening, then returning between half-ten and eleven, when he'd call out to be let back in. Then he'd sit up smoking with one of his fellow officers before turning in to bed about half-twelve.

Despite his title, the duties of the master gunner, Sergeant William Whitworth, were of a light nature, primarily associated with looking after the stores. This gave him plenty of time to tend his garden and the small orchard of fruit trees that grew along the inside of the fort embankment. Whitworth was just shy of forty years old, originally from Leeds, *(though I've no idea how thick his Yorkshire accent was),* and married, with children. *Six* children.

It was said that he'd seen hard service abroad, and now just wanted to serve out his time, another eight or nine months till he'd completed his twenty-one years and could take his pension. He'd been in the fort since July the year before, two or three months after the birth of his youngest child, Robert — little Bobbie — over on the mainland, in Alverstoke, near Gosport.

By all accounts, Whitworth was an odd fellow — something to do with the wound he'd taken to the head whilst on active service, perhaps? Almost as soon as Captain Manners had arrived, he learned that Whitworth had a grudge against the officers of the Royal Engineers in particular — "I've got something on them, you know, forged papers, enough to see them transported, colonels and generals alike" — and that he was also worried about losing his position before his pension date came due.

## Part the Third - the Days Before

It's Wednesday, May 16th, 1860. An itinerant razor-grinder, in from Newport, crosses the bridge into the fort, crosses the courtyard to Whitworth's house, and knocks on the door. A few minutes later, he's sharpening a razor against a sharpening stone. Mrs Eastley — Emma — wife to Corporal Eastley, of the Royal Engineers, crosses the courtyard with a pair of scissors to be sharpened.

The Eastleys had arrived at Sandown with their family the previous Thursday, although they'd also been stationed there for ten weeks or so at the end of last year. Although they live opposite the Whitworth's, they don't really know them: as a family, the Whitworths largely keep themselves to themselves. Emma Eastley would see Mrs. Whitworth — *Martha* — standing at the doorway every so often, and the eldest daughters, Molly, the eldest, and the other one, going into the village to run errands. But that was about it. At night time, she's seen all the children — though she doesn't know their names, the other children's, only Molly's — dressed in their nightclothes, peering out through both the upstairs windows opposite, as they made themselves ready for bed. If she'd been able to see right into the room, the one on the left, she'd have maybe noticed the mantle-shelf, and on it, a lead pencil, two prayer-books, and several other family knick knacks.

The grinder finishes the razor, wipes the white handle clean, and pops it back inside the Whitworths' house. Then does the scissors. That evening, Emma smiles across the courtyard to Mrs Whitworth, who's stood at the door. Later that night, she sees the whole family through the window, the fire burning in the fireplace, the baby in Martha's arms, or maybe at her breast.

The following day, the Thursday, was a strange day. That morning Emma Eastley found something like soapsuds mixed with the water in the cistern in the court yard, and remarked upon it, and that she hadn't really seen the children since then. Whitworth had definitely been acting a bit odd over the last few days too — "not quite consistent with soundness of intellect" would be one way of putting it. Only that day, he'd come out of his house in a hurry, and he wasn't right; his trousers were on wrong, for a start, not pulled over his boots. But he wasn't *dangerous*, he wasn't posing any sort of *threat* to anyone, least of all his family.

She'd also seen the second eldest daughter — Lizzie, was that her name? Elizabeth? — heading out that morning, crossing the yard to the fort gate, putting on her bonnet and shawl as she did so, nothing really strange about that, but she couldn't understand *why* the fire didn't appear to have been lit — there was no smoke coming from the chimney — or why the blinds hadn't been opened all day. And later that day, that night, she hadn't notice any light coming from the Whitworths' house at all, or, at least, not before she'd gone to bed, about ten *(British Summer Time didn't start till 1916, so today's equivalent would be 11pm now)*.

Bevis, Captain Manner's servant, was also around the fort that Thursday afternoon. He lived in the house adjoining Whitworth's, and spoke to him about three that afternoon — "the weather seems to be clearing up, I reckon?" — then watched him cross the square and go onto ramparts. Not long after, ten minutes perhaps, Lizzie came home, back into the fort, and into the house.

Bevis locked the gate, as he normally did, at half-nine, and walked back across the courtyard, noticing a flicker of downstairs light through a corner of his neighbour's blinds. When he went back out, just before eleven —  "Bevis, where are you? Let me in," followed by the sound of Captain Manners, returned from dinner at the Star and Garter, kicking at the locked gate — the house next door was all quiet, and in darkness.

"Is Mr Puckle in?" *(one of the other officers)*. "Yes, sir." "Okay, then; shut the door, and go to bed." And with that, Captain Manners crossed the courtyard, and went to see Puckle for his nightcap smoke.

## Part the Fourth - that Friday

It's Friday morning, the 18th, a sunny May day. The fruit trees, we might imagine, are in full blossom; spring is in the air.

But even so, it's surprising not to see smoke curling out of the chimney of the Whitworth house. And why are the shutters still shut when it's gone past eleven in the morning?

"What d'ya reckon?" joked Bevis, to his fellow servants. "I've a good mind to go and knock at the house and ask for summat to see if they're up. I shouldn't wonder that the master's made away with himself and the whole family!"

*But you shouldn't really joke about things like that...*

It's gone past one. Half past. Quarter to two. Emma Eastley is stood at her window: "why d'you think there's no fire on again today?" "I dunno." Her husband joins her: "but look, see, they're getting up: the shutter's moving", as the middle leaf of the shutter is opened up.

Whitworth leaves the house, his army great coat on, buttoned up to the neck, up to the top, right up to the chin. He leaves the fort, crosses the drawbridge, goes up past the Kings' Head, and continues up the hill, up towards the Star and Garter, and the barracks.

As he walks, his hand is held up to his neck. He appears to be stumbling.

"You all right? You okay? Anything wrong?" It's the chief boatman of the Sandown Coastguard Station. Whitworth's reply is a babble, incoherent. "It's horrid, horrid. They've got down the chimney, in my house; they've murdered my wife, and my children." He certainly is an odd one, this one, getting crazier by the day. "They have, they have, I've been watching them for two or three nights; there was a man standing down there, with a pistol in each hand; and they're all dead; my poor wife; my poor children; all my children." The coastguardsman shakes his "Don't talk nonsense. I mean, your neck: are *you* okay?" There's a smear of blood, at the side of the chin.

"It's nothing, it's nothing. Just a scratch... I've.. I've got to go up to the barracks; I'll see you when I get back down".

"Crazy man, crazy..." and the coastguardsman watches Whitworth carry on up the hill, turns to look down, towards the fort, back up to Whitworth, then shakes his head, and starts to walk down to the fort.

Up at the barracks, it's coming on two, and the bugle is sounded, as it does every day. The men are assembled in rank, the officers looking on from the steps outside their quarters.

Whitworth stumbles his way past the Star and Garter, up towards the parade ground.

The officers watch as he hurries across the parade ground, staggering, stumbling, making his way towards them.

He approaches two of the Captains, Robinson and Manners, and falls to his knees. He's babbling excitedly, his words barely comprehensible.

"For God's sake, help me, sir, for God's sake, preserve me!" He gets up, and with his right hand takes an envelope, stuffed with papers, out of his breast pocketl hands them over to Robinson, along with his watch, and some money. When they read the letter later, they see it's from Whitworth himself, to the adjutant, requesting permission to stay at the fort till he reaches his pension time.

"He's used me dreadfully; he's held a pistol at my head; and he swore he'd shoot me if I didn't cut my throat"; and he, Whitworth,  ... pulled ... down ... the collar, of his coat; and showed his throat: cut, and bloody. "There's awful work down there, awful work. Go down there. You *must* go down there."

Whitworth pushed past the officers, into their quarters, into the officers' mess. The men watched from the parade ground, saw Whitworth coming bak out again, his face and hands now very obviously covered with blood, led by some of the officers, off towards the hospital building.

The staff sergeant, John Daish, is ordered down to the fort. "Run, man, run. I said *hurry*. Find out just what the hell's going on down there." And Daish sets off, down the hill, back past the Star and Garter, back past the King;s Head, towards the fort.

Over the bridge, inside the fort, the Coastguardsman — partly against his better judgement, but partly... *concerned* — asks Corporal Eastley: "did you see the gunner, what's his name?" "Whitworth? Yeah, he just went by."

"Which are his quarters?" "They're... here, I'll show you," and he leads the Coastguardsman thirty five paces across the small courtyard.

The door was closed, but not locked.

The Coastguardsman opened the door, stepped inside, looked around, and immediately stepped outside to raise the alarm.

## Part the Fifth — Inside the House

Staff Sergeant Daish, along with two others, entered the courtyard, saw Corporal Eastley and his wife standing there, opposite Whitworth's house, saw the Coastguard man come out just as he was making his way to go in. "Have you seen his family? How are they?" "I... No...I... don't know.".

Daish pushed past the Coastguardsman, into the house — the living room. To the left, a staircase, to the right, another, then a door, to another downstairs room. Crockery on the dresser, two chairs in front of the fireplace, a printed children's frock hanging over each, yesterday's washing, put there to dry. A cut-throat razor, still in its case. Official looking papers, with spattereed dark stains, strewn across the floor... And a deal table, looking somehow like it's been knocked out of place.

But that's not what Daish noticed. Not what the Coastguardsman noticed.

They'd noticed the blood: the bloodstains, and the bloody footprints. An adult's, unusually shaped, some barefooted, some stockinged, from the webbed pattern, but still from the same oddly distinctive shaped feet; and a child's. At least one child's.

On the walls of the stairs, to the right, a few spots of blood. To the left...

Daish raced across the room, followed the footsteps — adult's, and child's — up the left hand staircase. Blood on the walls.

At the top, a bedroom, in darkness, the windows closed, the shutters covering them, except for a gap, six inches or so, in the right hand shutter. Even so, a smell, a terrible smell. Like a slaughterhouse.

Daish went to the partly opened shutter, and opened it fully. Looked round, not noticing the large blood spot on the window sill.

As he told it, the first thing he saw was an old sword — an old, curved naval cutlass — lying in the middle of the floor. The next thing, the ring on the woman's hand, dangling over the edge of the bed against the opposite wall. At right angles to it, another bed.

If he'd had to tidy up the floor, right there, right then, he'd have picked up an overturned bedchamber candlestick; the sword, of course, blood spotted, though possibly not "used", now you come to look at it properly; and a few items of children's clothing — isn't it always the same, clothes strewn across the floor of a child's bedroom?

Some blood soaked papers, one of which, if he'd taken the time, to read at the time, he'd have noticed was written in lead pencil, in Whitworth's own hand: "Some plot.—Sandown Fort, 1860, *17* May. Sergeant Whitworth and his wife and six children murdered." The handwritten scrawl also mentioned several officers as "rascals under cloaks as friends". Another, dated 13th May, to Captain Rogan, RA, signed by Whitworth, respectfully requesting, given a notice he'd received about being removed from Sandown Fort the next month, that his application to remain at Sandown till the expiration of his time for pension (another nine months or so) be forwarded to authorities.

Also on the floor, several official looking keys: a large one, and some smaller ones attached. And an open, white handled razor, it's edge blunted, and slightly turned, as if it had come in contact with something hard.

And if he'd then had to tidy the rest of the room?

The bed clothes were half on, half-off, the bed opposite the window. Laying obliquely across it, Mrs Whitworth — Martha — partly clothed in a dark blue dress, though without her stockings or nightcap on. Her arm is hanging over edge of the bed, the glitter of a ring on her finger. Her feet are bare. In her arms, or perhaps he had been at her breast, the baby, or maybe we should call him the toddler, little Bobby, in his nightdress. Laying across her right hand, another child, Freddy, aged six, "*and three quarters*", as he might have said, curled up, or maybe, if you look again, *doubled* up. At the mother's feet — Martha's feet — at the foot of the bed, her middle daughter, Lizzie, her head.... All the children, in nightdresses.

On the other bed — they didn't all sleep in this room normally, did they? If you'd gone to the other room, the one reached from the stairs to the *right*, rather than to the *left*, you'd have noticed a bedstead with some bedding on it, but no bed clothing. But they weren't in that room, they were in this. On that second bed? The three other children.

By the wall, at the head of the bed, the three year old — "I'm not three, I'm three *and a half*" — the one they called Willie. He looks peaceful, his eyes half opened, what looks like a smile across his face, as if he's asleep. But his fist is clenched. And his body is cold. Stiff. As are they all. "Fifteen or sixteen hours dead", the doctor would say. Next to the young boy, his slightly older sister, the youngest daughter, Ellen. She's laying on her back, her legs drawn up. Next to her, the eldest sister, christened as Mary Ann, but known as Molly, laying on her side, across the bed, as if she's been thrown there, her hands clenched, her features convulsed, her toes protruding through her blood soaked stockings, her feet the size of the footprints downstairs.

She must have tried to escape whatever had happened in that room, maybe taken one of the others with her — some of the stories say there were *two* sets of children's footsteps, down there in the kitchen — maybe tried to open the front door and make an escape — there was blood on the latch, and also on the door jamb — maybe hid under the deal table, knocking it out of place — maybe tried to go up to other set of stairs, the ones to the right, but then run back up here, or been dragged back up here, to have her windpipe severed by a blow from blunted instrument.

*And as you look around the room again, you see them all lying there, lying on the beds, each with their throat cut.*

`Look again at the second bed, the marks on the pillows, from being placed over the children's mouths, stifling their cries even if not suffocating them. Look at Ellen, her throat cut. At young William, his night dress and pillow blood stained, from the small, but deep, cut in his gashed throat.`

`Look again, at the first bed, the one against the wall, opposite the window. A sickening wound in Mrs Whitw... in *Martha's* throat, her windpipe completely severed, along with larger vessels and nerves; the wound is six inches long, and deep, *very* deep, right down to the bone on both sides. That would surely have blunted any sharp razor. She'd have been dead in two minutes or less. And she must have been killed in her sleep — there are no cuts on hands or even any signs of resistance. She hopefully never saw the baby, little Bobby, dragged up by the hair on his very own head to have his throat cut, his throat sawed. Or Freddy, perhaps he'd been thrown onto the bed, perhaps he'd been the one who'd made it briefly downstairs with his biggest sister, before having his throat cut from ear to ear by some jagged instrument. Or Lizzie, perhaps she'd had some hope of escape — her naked feet were blooded too — before having her head half severed from her body by a wound eight inches in length.`

## Part the Sixth — In Hospital

Back up at the Barracks, Whitworth was taken into the hospital wing in something of a precarious state. The local surgeon, Dr Leeson, washed his cut throat — two self-inflicted wounds, they seemed to be, one a mere scratch, the second a deep cut, as if done in desperation using a blunt instrument, such as a razor with a blunted edge — and bandaged it with lint. He also cleaned Whitworth's feet, which were quite distinctive in shape — a large bunion on one, and some odd shaped protuberances on the side of each: there were covered with blood. And then he went down to the fort.

That evening, his mind wandering, Whitworth occasionally made mention of his wife in affectionate and touching terms— "she's a good woman, a good wife. I nursed her once, for a fortnight, night and day, and she nursed me; and I nursed the children too..." Then more rambling... "They're coming, the ships, they're coming to attack the fort." And again: "is it condemned, the fort?"

From the doctor, checking the bandage on his head: "is it comfortable?" "Yes, yes it's comfortable," and off he went again: "ah, my poor wife, she used to put a great many bandages on my head. She was a good woman, my wife, a good woman."

It's midnight, that Friday night, in the hospital. "What are you doing here?" "Keeping you company." "Oh poor Bobby, poor little Bobby is gone." "What are they called, your children?" "The eldest is Molly; then there's Lizzie, and Ellen; and Freddy, and Willie; and Bobby; poor little Bobby; poor little Bobby is gone." "How old's your eldest?" "Twelve; she's... twelve." "And the youngest?" "I.. twelve months maybe?" And he started rambling again, about the fort, about his garden...

Not long after, whilst still in the hospital, it's said he confessed to murders; and several rumours started that he'd also intended to blow up fort, but had lost the key... "And the powder in the magazine was all piled together in centre, you know..."

*There's another story I know about a gunner, and a fort, that took place almost forty years later, in July 1900, at Brean Down, overlooking Weston-Super-Mare. I first heard of it as a song, "The Tale of Gunner Haines", by Gaz Brookfield, did the storynotes research, pulled the tale together from contemporary news reports of the time, but never got it to work. Gaz Brookfield's version is perfect — a really efficient telling. It tells of how Gunner Haines went out for a drink one night, punctured his bicycle on the way home and got back after curfew, for which he was placed on a charge. This seemed to have tipped him over the edge, because the next thing anyone knew was the sound of an explosion of the fort's magazine going up, taking Gunner Haines with it. I commend it to you.*

There were other rumours too, and gossip, flying from lip to lip, house to house, from village to village, town to town ...

The intention to blow up the magazine rumour, of course — *how could they give the key to a raving madman?* — but also that one of the children had been suffocated, or that two of the bodies had been run through with the sword (so why wasn't there a post mortem?" opined the Isle of Wight Observer, "that would have settled a lot of the questions...")

And the wedged piece of wood that had been found in house, and surely used to secure the door to stop the children making their escape?

"And didn't you know, the eldest girl, what was her name, Molly, or the other one, Lizzie, I heard they'd said, to someone in the village, that she was too scared to go home, and..." "Yes, yes, I heard that too, they'd been saying it for days..."

And really, it just wasn't good enough that there were no guards posted, that the servants, the *servants* had charge of the keys after dark, and that the only way back in to the fort after hours was for the officers to shout and holler for the servants, and then give the door a good kicking...

"He was going to be kicked out, you know... he'd had the letter, saying he was being let go, and him all hopeful of getting his pension..."

But one of the biggest questions was, how could it have happened without anybody hearing? The fort was only small, and there were at least eight other people living there — the officers, their servants, and the Eastleys. It couldn't have been done quietly, and whoever had made it into the kitchen must surely have tried to call for help?

## Part the Seventh — Due Process

It's the mess room in the officer's house at the fort the following day, Saturday, May 19th, 1860. One o'clock in the afternoon.

A foreman is elected, and the Coroner's Jury is sworn in. The army have sent an observer, and nine reporters are in attendance. *Nine.* This is already a big story.

The Jury walk across the small courtyard, thirty five paces square, to observe the bodies, and when they're done, the police apply to the Board of Guardians of the Newport Union to have the bodies buried the next day.

Back in the mess, it's made clear to the Jurymen that it's *not* for them to decide on the state of mind of the murderer — there was already no doubt about who was responsible — just whether or not it *was* a case of murder. The Judge further instructed the Jury to ignore anything they might already have heard about the case, and judge it purely on the basis of the evidence presented to them, also bearing in mind that Whitworth was in no fit state to be brought before them.

After a hearing lasting six hours, during which time some of the jurors had to leave the room in tears, and one had completely fainted away during the testimony of the surgeon, the Judge started his summing up about 7pm, and took about an hour to do so. The only duty of the Jury then, was to say whether they believed murder had been committed and whether Whitworth was likely innocent or guilty of it. *Not* whether he was insane. The Jury went out at 8pm and returned at just before nine with a unanimous decision: *wilful murder*.

Outside the court, rumours had continued to spread. "She was a hard working woman, you know. Honest. And careful." "She knew he wasn't right that night." "She was scared, you know, she knew something was up, made sure she had all her children with her that night." "Such a shame she nodded off.." "You know there was a stick, she was laying on a stick. She was ready for him." "If only she hadn't fallen asleep..."

The Coroner's papers were signed off on the Monday. The jurors had wanted it noted that, *in their opinion*, Whitworth was of unsound mind: "please, for the sake of our neighbours, and the locality, just let us say that", but the coroner was adamant, said it wouldn't have any weight un the process, it wouldn't play any part in Whitworth's trial, because that wasn't for the Coroner's Court to determine.

Later that night, of Monday the 21st of May, the night before he would be transported to Winchester gaol, WHitworth was questioned again: "that old devil, he was standing in the corner of the room, with a slouched hat on, a loaded pistol in his hand. 'If you don't cut their throats, I'll shoot you, and I won't be long about it'. I'd been watching, you know, I'd been watching for him the last two or three nights; there'd been a nasty smell down the chimney, and I'd sat up to watch it, and my wife too, I got her to watch with me..."

The following day, the Tuesday, Whitworth was sent to Winchester gaol. Still in a feeble state, he was taken, handcuffed, in a fly by two constables, Ingram and Edson. When they got to Ryde, he was placed in a Bath chair and wheeled along the pier, then put on board a steam packet for Portsmouth, and from there on to Winchester.

He waits eight weeks, till July 17th, a Tuesday, 1860, for his trial to come on at the  Winchester Summer Assizes. he was indicted under his full name — *William Henry Whitworth*, a soldier, aged 39 — for the wilful murder of Martha, his wife, and their six children, Frederick, William, Robert, Mary Ann, Elizabeth, and Ellen, at Sandown, in the parish of Brading, on the Isle of Wight.

He stared vacantly around the court, muttering incoherently to himself, apparently eating something as he did so. A tall man, he looked much older than his age would admit, *very grey*, I've heard it said, a wild and vacant look in his eye. It was thought necessary that he should be closely guarded.

Although the trial was scheduled as a murder trial, there was an important question that needed to be decided upon first: was Whitworth in a fit state to understand the charge made against him?

"Decidedly not, my lord," was the opinion of the prison doctor, Dr. Lyford. Whitworth had been in his charge since he'd arrived at Winchester in late May, since when he'd been in daily and constant attendance. In his opinion, Whitworth's mental powers were "almost extinct, arising from a softening of brain caused by a disease that's been going on for some time." The damage was "probably permanent", and the doctor was confident in his opinion that Whitworth was quite insane.

Aware that *something* was going on, and much amused at the appearance of the court, Whitworth placed his arm around neck of turnkey stood next to him, pointed to his lordship, the Judge, and smiled.

"He clearly wouldn't know the difference between guilty, and not guilty."

"I do not believe he would, my lord."

"Then I will impannel a jury to try *that* question, as to whether the prisoner can understand the pleadings to an indictment." And the jury found that he couldn't.

Although there would be no case to plead — no *guilty* or *not guilty* verdict to find — no *sentence*, as such, to pass — there was still a determination to be made. "The effect of your finding that he is unable to plead, on the ground of insanity or aberration of mind, or a deficiency of mental intelligence, will be that he will be ordered to be confined during Her Majesty's pleasure `[Victoria was already on the throne for almost twenty five years, had been since 1837]`, in order to see what the result will be. He will not, of course, as you may suppose, be allowed to go at large, for his doing so would be extremely dangerous. He will remain in safe custody during Her Majesty's pleasure, and be properly treated for the disease under which he labours."

The gaoler motioned for the prisoner to leave the bar, but Whitworth didn't seem to understand. He was eventually led away by the arm, at which point he started loudly exclaiming to the Court, "Gracious God, look down upon you all, you miserable sinners."

## Part the Eighth - Remembering

It's October, 2026. I first came across this story a couple of years ago, by way of hunt for an original version of the Michael Morey rhyme, which I managed to find in a mid-19th version:

"Michael Morey he is dead,  
For he cut off his grandson's head;  
So he hung on Arreton Down,  
Where he shall stay till he drops down."

It was in an archived copy of the *Isle of Wight Observer*, dated Saturday, 9th March, 1861, in an article entitled *"Murder in the Isle of Wight*.

The article also included an intriguing sentence: *As to the Sandown tragedy of last year, it was committed by a Leeds soldier who might have been quartered in Canterbury or Dublin just likely as where be was; and even he was a maniac, and never tried.*

And it *was* a tragedy. As you've heard, *a terrible tal*e. But one I'd never heard before, and perhaps for good reason.

There is a story that can't be told, around Ross-on-Wye, where we often go, or at least, not in the Forest (the Forest of Dean): *"don't mention the bears"* is the watchword, the watch*phrase*. Because if you *do* mention the bears, then as likely as not, you'll start a fight. A fight prompted by *shame*. And I wonder if that's partly why, at least, the tale of the Sandown tragedy *has* been forgotten, or at least, not spoken of. Because of the shameful way that Martha Whitworth, née Beech, twelve year old Mary Ann — *Molly*, ten year old Elizabeth — *Lizzie*, six year old Frederick — "six* and three quarters*" — *Freddy*, five year old *Ellen*, three year old *William* — "I'm three *and a half*" — the namesake of his father, but known to them all as Willie, and little baby Robert, little Bobby, just eighteen months of age, were treated, not just by the person who should have cared for them, but the person who should have *protected* them, but instead who put an end to their lives, their *brief lives*, their *very* brief lives, but also the authorities, and the people, who didn't, in death, give them the courtesy of a decent burial — what happened to the penny subscription collected for their memorial stone? — or the promise of eternal rest under a remembrance of their own names. For the crime committed against them was not their crime. And they don't deserve to be buried unrecognised.
