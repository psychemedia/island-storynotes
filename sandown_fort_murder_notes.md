# Sandham Fort Murder notes


May 1860, Sandown Fort (Fort Street, Sandham Gardens); had previously been up for sale to the Nobility and Gentry in 1831 as a desirable residence with a unique character and stunning sea views;

Sergeant William Henry Whitworth [rumoured not actually a sergeant]

Names are confused in some or reporting - Molly and Ellen, based on what Daish said he'd heard Whitworth call the children; or misremembered eldest for youngest;

(From wedding and baptismal papers in house) wife Martha (née Beach) 6 children (3 boys, 3 girls) : Mary Ann (Molly, ~12), Elizabeth, (Lizzie, >9), Ellen (~4), Frederick (Freddy, <6), William (Willie, ~2-3), and Robert (Bobby) (+ another, ?, living away from home); family seemed "united and affectionate"; others in fort thought 7, one child lived away, but no evidence of that;

- Sandown a romantic village and holiday destination, craggy undercliff, and summer glories
- Coast Brigade of Royal Artillery, stationed at Sandown Fort, Isle of Wight; master-gunner in charge of the Fort since July 1859; aged about 35; of Leeds;
- principal duties of light nature, connected with stores, perhaps as a sop to previous good conduct and useful service;
- house situated in precinct of fort; said to have seen hard service abroad, where wounded in head; actions at Sandown were quiet and reserved; with wife, "kept themselves to themselves", averse to gossip; perfectly harmless and peaceable family; among colleagues, Whitworth regarded as being an "odd man" (and maybe not particularly liked); appeared to have grudge against certain class of engineer officer, on one occasion telling captain he had s/thing on them in form of forged letters in his possession, which captain ignored on basis all old soldiers grumbled about their superiors.
- small fort, home to Isle of Wight Artillery; in a field somewhat near the sea-shore, reached by a left hand turning  deviating from Ventnor high road (from Brading/Ryde, presumably); moat bridge approach to fort, then gate leading to moderate-sized court; humble dwelling-houses to right and left for NCOs and their families; officers quarters facing the gate;
- Whitworth's house small two storied cottage, rather dilapidated, on left side of court, v. small apartments/rooms: two on ground floor, one containing front door into court, another to garden, tended with great care, at back and foot of earthen rampart; Mrs Whitworth striven hard to make it as comfortable a home as humble means allowed; stair cases to right and left to upstairs bedrooms, but only one on right appears to have been used for sleeping (?differ to going up left stairs?); room very small, 16 feet square; two bedsteads of equal size, one farthest from the window placed in a longitudinal direction, and the other near the window; shutters, made of three or four folding leaves, were closed; 
- barracks at top of Sandown (Heights/Poo museum), where another part of Isle of Wight Artillery stationed;  at stated times day, different companies drill;
- keen to stay at Sandown Fort, just 8 or 9 months till his pension term (for completing 21 years service), but worried he would be turned out;
- recent conduct somewhat strange, "not quite consistent with soundness of intellect"; acting a bit "odd", but not thought to be dangerous to self or family; Thursday, put on his trousers oddly (not pulled over his boots).

- one rumour as to cause of insanity, alleged complaints made to head-quarters respecting him, removal from position was imminent; accounts were in order / balanced, no "pecuniary embarrassments"
- Thursday — some of family had been seen;
- Friday morning, calm sunshine of a bright May day, spring budding all around, neighbours opposite noticed house seemed quiet; no smoke from chimney; blinds closed;
- late Thursday/early Friday, 17/18 May, 1860;  nothing was known of it until a short time after noon on Friday; observed by coast-guardsman (Hoel) on duty in a lane adjacent to Fort, between King's Head and Walkyier's (? Star and Garter Hotel), walking towards parade ground, hand to his neck; very rambling and incoherent conversation (rumoured that he'd said "some one has got down the chimney in my house, and murdered my wife and children, though that didn't appear in Coroner's inquest reports); "what's wrong?"  put his hands to his head, and said, "Oh, horrid; they've murdered my six children." "Don't talk nonsense." "They have, they have, I've been watching these men two or three nights; there was a man standing down there, with a pistol in each hand; and they're all dead—my poor wife; my children; my poor children; all my children."  "What's the matter with you? Are *you* okay?" gesturing neck, which looked like it was bleeding. "Nothing, just a scratch"; "I have to go up to the barracks; I'll see you when I come back down"; in one report Coastguardsman took no notice (Whitworth was mad), but it actually seems he was concerned enough to start to make his way to Whitworth's house at the fort, and saw Eastley, who lived opposite. "Did you see the gunner, what's his name?" "Yeah, he just went by." "Which are his quarters?" "They're.. I'll show you.." The door was closed but not locked. Opened the door, looked round, saw tracks of blood on the floor and stairs. Immediately went out and raised an alarm, saw Sergeant Daish and two others, told them what was inside...
- Whitworth went up to barracks; bugle sounded about two o'clock, as per custom, and men assembled in rank with officers; Whitworth, clothed in soldier's overcoat, collar covering his neck, seen rushing towards Captains Robinson and Manners, fell to knees before them, wild actions / hurried expressions; suspicion raised, some officers men sent rushing down to fort / house; as he rambled, severe wound noticed on his throat, tho' not deep enough to cause instantaneous death; - moved to hospital in precarious state; doctors washed his throat, put on lint; feet washed to cleanse blood; doctor also later inspected them to match distinguishing features in footprints from the house; that evening, mind wandering, occasionally mentioning wife in affectionate/ touching terms—  "a good woman, a good wife"— and children. "I nursed her for a fortnight once night and day, and she nursed him; and I nursed the children" More rambling... "they're coming, the ships, they're coming to attack the fort." "Is it condemned, the fort?" From the doctor, checking the the bandage on his head: "is it comfortable?" "Yes, yes it's comfortable; ah my poor wife, she used to put a great many bandages on my head. She was a good woman, my wife, a god woman."
- while in hospital confessed to murders; rumours he also intended to blow up fort, but had lost the key
- midnight, Friday, in hospital, to person in charge: "what are you doing here?" "Keeping you company." "Poor little Bobby is gone." "What are they called, your children?" "The eldest is Ellen; then there's Lizzie, and Molly, and Freddy,  and Willie; and Bobby; poor little Bobby is gone." `[Names are wrong order?]` "How old's the eldest one?" "Twelve; she's twelve." "And the youngest?" "I.. twelve months maybe?" Also rambled about the fort, and his garden.
- couldn't sleep night Mon 21 May, when questioned said "that that old ——— was standing in the corner of the room with a slouched hat on, with a loaded pistol in his hand, and said, if you don't cut all their throats directly, I will shoot you, and I will not be long about it. I had been watching some two or three nights, and there had been a nasty disagreeable smell down the chimney, and I had set up to watch it, and I had got my wife to watch too."
- sent to Winchester gaol, on Tues 22 May 1860, to await his trial, following verdict of Coroner's court being signed on Monday, 21 May, 1860; although in a feeble state. taken handcuffed in a fly by constables Ingram and Edson, n arrival at Ryde, placed in a Bath chair, wheeled along pier, and put on board steam packet for Portsmouth, then Winchester

- sergeant went in the direction of the fort ?mentioned it to 2 NCOs, because they went to Whitworth's house: dreadful scene: wife & children, throats cut ("nearly severing their heads from their bodies"; ? one of the children's heads being severed from the body) with razor; NCOs left place undisturbed and sent for police; floor saturated with blood, smelt like a slaughterhouse.

- similar marks on stairs
- bedroom dark, windows closed, shutters covering them; right hand shutter open about six inches;
- a pencil on the mantle-shelf, two prayer-books, other little family articles
- old sword (long naval cutlass) lying on floor in middle of room among blood soaked papers, blood spotted but didn't look to have been used; also an open, white handled razor (edge was turned as if it had come in contact with something hard), covered with blood, 18"/2' from Mrs Whitworth's bed, opp. her head; some items of childrens' clothing
- one of the papers, written in lead pencil, in Whitworth's hand "Some plot."—Sandown Fort, 1860, 17 May. Sergeant Whitworth and his wife and six children murdered." also mentioning several officers as "rascals under cloaks as friends". Another, dated 13 May, to Captain Rogan, RA, signed by Whitworth, respectfully requesting, given a notice he'd received about being removed from Sandown Fort next month, that his application to remain  at Sandown till expiration of time for pension (~9 months) be forwarded to authorities.
- terrible scene in bedroom; blood all over floor, wall, bed post, large spot on window sill, blood soaked into beds; two beds, six bodies, three on each bed; all covered up, and appeared to have been murdered without being disturbed, bodies laying rather than thrown onto beds; bed clothes disordered, partly on floor; several keys, a large one and small ones attached (one of them rumoured to be the key of the magazine)
- all family except mother in night clothes, five of children wearing socks; opinion was unsuspecting children slumbering sleep of confiding innocence when deed was done; no appearance of struggling.
- on bed furthest from window, against wall leading to window, opp. stairs, mother, Mrs. Harris, partly undressed (dark blue dress, without stockings or cap), in oblique position, arm hanging over edge of the bed, glitter of ring on her finger, feet bare, a sickening wound in throat (windpipe completely severed, and large vessels and nerves; wound six inches long, very deep, down to the bones on both sides; dead in two minutes or less; by cutting to bone of woman with razor, it may have jagged/blunted/dulled razor); (it's said a stick was also found under her body, perhaps placed their for her own protection); presumably killed while asleep - no cuts on hands nor any signs of resistance; youngest babe (Robert / Bobbie; 4 months) in her arms/ at the breast, suffocated (but coroner says male, about 1 year old; cut in its throat, apparently sawed by the sword, held child up by hair of its head at the time); on her right hand, another child (Frederick, male, 4/5 yrs), curled up / doubled up in its own blood, throat cut from ear to ear with a jagged instrument (carried there and thrown on bed, as there was a great quantity of blood in centre of room?); At her feet / foot of bed, head on a pillow, lay [second] eldest girl (Elizabeth), with her head half severed from body, wound being eight inches in length, also hacked (by sword?); blood on her naked feet (mother's?)
- On second bed, nearest window, three more children in similar condition (eldest daughter (Molly, 11/12/13 yrs),  laying across bed on her side, as if thrown on the bed, clenched hands, convulsed features), windpipe severed by blow from blunt instrument (sword?); stockings covered with blood, toes protruding through them, corresponding to footsteps in kitchen; a small boy also lying across bed, in night clothes, (William, 18 months/2yrs); toddler lying on pillow,  eyes half opened, placid attitude, a kind of smile, as if asleep, except for little marble hand, stiffened and clenched by side, the gashed throat, (a small, but deep wound), possibly first, with razor, in its sleep, and blood stained night dress and pillow; other girl, (Ellen, 4yrs), similar cut across her throat, lay on her back, with her legs drawn up; marks on pillows indicated when they struggled, pillows placed over their mouths, to stifle cries, if not suffocate them;
- from blood stains on stockings of eldest daughter, and footprints on stairs and kitchen floor, seems she must have run down stairs, crouched under a deal table at further end of room, then chased by her father back up stairs; from  marks in the rooms below and on the stairs, appeared that some of children had escaped from room, then pursued and driven upstairs again,
- all bodies cold, stiff
- following the inquest, it started to be said, since the inquest was held, that the unfortunate wife (hard working, honest, careful mother), went to bed that night fearful that night, clustered children round her in one room, rather than the usual two; kept her clothes on, but nodded off; and further rumoured that a stick found under her body was for her own protection.
- later, another razor in case in kitchen

Inquest

- held in mess room of the officer's house of the fort, Saturday May 19, 1860, 1 o'clock; Coroner, F. Blake, Esq. Rev. C. Bury elected as Jury of foreman; Captain Cainers observing obo army. Jury sworn in, went to observe bodies;
- nine reporters present
- not for jury to decide state of mind of murderer, ignore anything they may have heard, purely judge the evidence
- several members of Coroner's Jury left the room in tears
- Whitworth not fit to be brought before Jury
- one juryman fainted at the testimony of the surgeon, Leeson
- lasted six hours, summing up 7pm for an hour; the only duty of the Jury then present was to say whether they believed the unfortunate man was innocent or guilty of these horrible acts, not whether he was insane. Jury went out at 8pm and returned at just before nine o'clock - unanimous decision, wilful murder.
- papers signed off on the Monday, jurors wanting it noted that in their opinion Whitworth was of unsound mind, "for the sake of their neighbours and the locality", coroner saying that would have no weight because that wasn't in their power;


Burial

- (before Weds 23 May, 1860) board guardians of Newport Union applied to by police to bury bodies
- Funeral Sunday, 20th, 1-2000 people assembled;
- sent over seven boxes, mother's stained black ("blackened over", a one disgusted correspondent wrote), and her children's white ("whitewashed")— scarcely to be designated coffins— into which remains indecently forced by pauper officials; some of coffins not large enough, bodies crushed into them with sacrilegious indecency, no funeral clothing, no attempt to perform normal offices; lids nailed on with a few common nails; piled House of Industry van/cart and sent at one o'clock to Brading Church for interment; arrived just before two o'clock; large crowd, including several men of Isle of Wight Militia Artillery, who volunteered to help move bodies into church, carried in on soldiers of Artillery men, and others, placed on 3 biers in centre aisle (mother and baby (Bobbie); two eldest girls (Molly and Lizzie); two other boys (Teddie and Willie) and youngest girl (Ellen)). Service read by vicar, Rev. Isidore? (D. J.?) Heath to densely crowded church / crammed full, hundreds came who could not obtain admission, but no relatives or friends. Interred in square grave, scarcely three feet deep, as indecent as all other aspects of funeral.
- solemn service concluded, procession reformed, proceeded to the grave, a vast hole of eight feet in width, five feet in depth, and seven feet in length, where remaining rites of church were performed, bodies lowered, amidst involuntary and unrestrained weepings and wailings of thousands there assembled;
- Vicar then addressed the multitude on necessity of controlling tempers, and resisting temptation
- many people flocked into the town, but, owing to the early hour of interment (2 o'clock) many were disappointed, it having been currently reported that the solemn ceremony would not take place until 4 o'clock
- funeral notice does not give any of the children's names
-a few condemnatory remarks from an individual present shouted ou, so indecorous, and out of place, Vicar prudently declined to enter into any discussion or controversy
- Mr. Beresford, an Independent minister, addressed the crowd from steps of the Old Town Hall in the market-place,
- a penny subscription suggested for erecting a memorial
- one correspondent to Isle of Wight Observer (in original incarnation, launched in 1845/5 for 30 issues, folded, relaunched in 1852 and ran till 1922), was unsympathetic to idea of a monument, seeing it as "a bloody leaf added to the hitherto unsullied history of our Island"; "why disturb the holy feelings which occupy the mind of a visitor when near the grave of "Jane, the Young Cottager,'" [Little Jane, of Annals of the Poor]. But the editor, and other correspondents, were firmly in facvour of the idea, one even trying to get the bodies exhumed so they could be re-interred in a more fitting way.

John Daish

- staff sergeant, Isle of Wight Artillery, stationed at Sandown
- identified woman and eldest 2 children
- last saw Mrs Whitworth at the door, 11am on Tues; talked with Whitworth Tues about gun drill, nothing strange in his manner
- on Barrack parade Friday, twenty past two. While on Parade Whitworth came towards Parade, near Star and Garter Hotel, very hurried / almost staggering across parade ground, towards officers' quarters. Several officers standing round door steps. Went to Captain Robinson, fell on knees before him. Talked in very excited manner, couldn't distinguish words; ( "For God's sake, sir, preserve me! (or Save me)"); got up, with right hand took some papers in envelope out of breast pocket and gave them Robinson, with his watch, [some said key], and some money; "He's used me dreadfully; he's held a pistol at my head, and swore he'd shoot me if I didn't cut my throat;"  "He's used me shamefully, and has held a pistol at my head, and swore he would shoot me if I did not cut my throat." pulled down the collar of his coat and showed his throat, cut and bloody.  "There's awful work down there, go down there, you must go down there." Pushed his way into the mess room with officers; then brought out again and taken to the hospital.
- "He can't have murdered the whole family, can he?" "You think?"
- Major Gordon called for some one to take Whitworth to hospital; accompanied him there with others. Face and hands bloody, soldier's overcoat/great coat buttoned close up chin, so couldn't see throat.
- By direction of Capt. Worsley, ran to Whitworth's house at fort to look for wife and children; saw Corporal Eastley (Royal Engineers), wife, standing in fort court-yard, opposite house. Knew nothing of what had occurred. When about to enter house, saw Coastguard man Hoare come out. "Have you seen his family? How are they?" "No...I don't know.". Went into house, blood on door jamb and latch of door, saw scene in kitchen, when upstairs, opened shutter, looked round, called out of window for assistance. Three men came up three men came up, a corporal (William Corney), a gunner, coastguardsman Hoare.
