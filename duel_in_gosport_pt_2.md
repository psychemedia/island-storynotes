SPRING ASSIZES. WESTERN CIRCUIT.-Winchester, March 2.

...

There is a report prevalent in the town that Lieutenant Hawkey, who, our readers may remember, was one of the principals in the unhappy duel which took place some months since, near Southampton; the result of which was the death of Captain Seton, will surrender to take his trial at the present assizes; but we believe the fact to be that Lieutenant Pym, who was Mr. Hawkey's second at the duel, will surrender and be tried, but that Mr. Hawkey will not do so at present. We understand that Mr. Cockburn and Mr. Sergeant Kinglake are retained for the defence. We need scarcely say that the above report has caused considerable excitement, as the parties are all so well known in the neighbourhood. Nothing of importance has taken place in the Crown Court to-day.

THE LATE DUEL AT PORTSMOUTH.

TRIAL OF LIEUTENANT PYM

WINCHESTER, Friday March 6.

This being the day fixed for the trial of Lieutenant Pym, for aiding and abetting the wilful murder of Captain Seton, the Court was densely crowded at an early hour. Precisely at nine o'clock the Learned Justice Erle took his seat in the Court.

Lieutenant Pym immediately surrendered to take his trial.

Mr. Parkson and Mr. W. Smith appeared on behalf of the prosecution; and Mr. Cockburn, Q.C., and Mr. Sergeant Kinglake for the defence.

The prisoner being called on to plead, pleaded not guilty in a firm voice; he appeared to be a young man of about twenty-two or twenty-three, and was of prepossessing and gentlemanly appearance.

Mr. Rawlinson stated the facts of the case to the Jury, but they are doubtless so fresh in the remembrance of our readers, and are so fully set out in the evidence, that it is useless to give a report of the Learned Gentleman's speech here. The indictment charged the defendant with being present, aiding and abetting one Henry Charles Morehead Hawkey in the wilful murder of ——— Seton, by shooting at him with a pistol loaded with gunpowder and a leaden bullet, and wounding him on the right side, near the hip. The first witness called was

Henry Hollingsworth, examined by Mr. W. Smith— Am one of the proprietors of the King's Rooms, at Portsea. There are evening assemblies there every Monday in the summer season. Capt. Seton and Mr. Hawkey were subscribers. On the 12th of May last there was an assembly, Hawkey and Seton were there. There is a card-room adjoining the end room. I saw Hawkey and Seton go in there. I did not hear what passed between them. They stayed there 10 minutes, and then came out into the ball-room. I did not observe anything particular about their manner. I saw them again about twelve. Soon after that I saw Mr. Hawkey speaking to Mr. Pym. They are both lieutenants of Marines. Mr. Hawkey's name is Henry Charles Morehead Hawkey. Hawkey left soon after twelve. Pym and Hawkey had been constant attendants at the rooms, but did not come after that night. Before Seton left I saw Hawkey and Pym sitting on a sofa near the door. Seton spoke to Mr. Hawkey as he went out of the room.

Cross-examined by Mr. Cockburn— I did not hear what passed in the card-room. I did not hear Seton say to Hawkey as he came out of the door-way, "You shall suffer for this." I heard no offensive expressions. I did not see Mr. Hawkey go up to Mrs. Seton. I saw Mrs. Hawkey in the ball-room at this time. Some time after I saw Mr. Seton speak to Mrs. Hawkey. When Seton spoke to Hawkey as he went out it was only for a moment. I did not see Hawkey make any movement with his foot. I cannot say he did not. Mrs. Hawkey was at this time in the dressing-room. I believe so. The rooms were full. I did not see Seton dancing with Mrs. Hawkey. I did not hear Seton say as he came out of the ball-room, "Pray, sir, go — quit — there are I ladies here." I saw Lieutenant Rowles go into the card-room with Seton just before he left.

Andrew Robert Savage, examined by Mr. Rawlinson — I am the acting adjutant of the Royal Artillery at Portsea, and was at the ball on the 17th of May. I was one of the stewards I saw Hawkey, Seton, and Pym. During the evening I took Hawkey into the card-room. I said I had been told he had called Seton a blackguard and a scoundrel, and had been asked to try and arrange the matter; I had refused to interfere, but that I had consented in my capacity of a steward to interfere. Hawkey said the matter could not be arranged, as he had received an injury and not an insult. He also said that Seton had said that light cavalry would not meet the infantry.

By the Judge— By that I understand you would not meet him in a duel. I saw Hawkey the next morning at nine. He said Captain Bowles had been sent to him by Seton to arrange a hostile meeting, and that arrangements had been made for that purpose. He said he had chosen his second. I said I was sorry he had chosen so young and inexperienced a man. He said that he did not know who else to ask. I had seen Seton dance twice with Mrs. Hawkey at this ball.

I do not know what the second dance was. When I spoke to Hawkey it was about ten minutes to twelve. I left just after. Mr. Rowles had told me that Seton had called Hawkey a blackguard and a scoundrel. I did not know the reason that Seton had for this. I would not allow him to I tell me. I had known Seton before. I soon after saw him walking with Mrs. Hawkey before this. Mr. Seton had been in a cavalry regiment. Hawkey was in the marines. Hawkey told me that Seton had sent him a challenge, and that Rowles was his second.

Examined by Mr. Rawlinson— I do not recollect having seen Seton alone with Mrs. Hawkey. I had never remarked anything particular between them.

John Luis Town, examined by Mr. Smith— I am a merchant residing at Portsmouth. On the evening of the 20th of May last I saw two gentlemen walking together, one was Hawkey. They were in the undress uniform of the marines. I cannot say that the prisoner was one of the men.

By the Judge — I cannot say he was not.

Did you hear any observation made by Mr. Hawkey to the other person? [Objected to, on the ground that it was not shown to be made in the presence of the prisoner. It was in the nature of an act done, and a declaration of or intention by Hawkey, and goes to show the justice existing in his mind at the time the pistol was fired]. I heard Mr. Hawkey say to his friend, "I will shoot him as I would a partridge." I did not hear his friend make any answer to this. They were going towards Portsmouth.

I laid a bet of 5*l*. with Lieutenant Pym that I would shoot with him, not knowing that I had not my own at my rooms; will you let me have a pair?" I showed him a pair. The barrels were eight or nine inches long, with hair triggers. They were duelling pistols. He asked to borrow them, but I said I could not lend them as they were new. By the Judge— As a marine; "a soldier officer, not a naval officer." I should say a marine. He asked if I had a shooting gallery. I said I had. He asked to be shown up to have a few shots. He went up. One of my men attended on him. This was George Powell. He came after I had shown the gentleman up stairs. After this I heard shots in the gallery. I do not know how many. I do not not recognise him at all — I do not know the man.

Cross-examined by Mr. Cockburn— I have been in the habit of seeing gentlemen practice for duelling. I have seen them practice in many ways. Gentlemen practice with a straight arm sometimes — sometimes turn round and shoot snap shots.

George Powell, examined by Mr. Rawlinson— I work for Mr. Sherwood. I remember, on the 20th of May last, a gentleman coming to my master's gallery to practice. He had a few shots, and went away. He afterwards came again with another gentleman. I should not know him again. They were in an undress marine uniform. When I they came a case of new pistols was brought. The case was eighteen inches long. They were duelling pistols— nine inch barrels. I believe it was a mahogany case. The pistols were fired four times. The gentleman who came first fired three shots, and the other one. I loaded them. The gentleman who came first asked me to let his friend see me load them. He stood the whole length of the gallery. It was not a good shot. The gentleman who came last said, "that would have done," and something else that I did not hear. When the man fired the last shot he said, "It was a damned good pistol." and took up a tool and marked an X on it. I received 3s.; 2s. for the bullets, 6d. for the shooting, and 6d. for myself. The gentleman who came first when he came said, "he had a match to shoot for 5*l.*" He shot in a plain practice shooting way.

Cross-examined by Sergeant Kinglake— There is another kind of shooting called snap shooting. In that they stand with the pistol hanging down by the side, one, two, three, is given, and then turn round quick and fire. The gentleman had fired both pistols off before he asked me to show his friend how to load them. I loaded them again, which made the four shots.  There were plenty more bullets and powder if the parties had wished to fire more.

By the Judge—The gentleman who came first fired both pistols once, and then the second one again, which, he marked.

By Mr. Rawlinson— When he went away he said he would send for them. I took them down stairs. I afterwards recollect some one took the pistols away the same day.

Mr. Rawlinson submitted that it was clear that upon the record as it stood, everything that would evidence against Hawkey was admissible.

Mr. Justice Erle said that the evidence was not admissible.

Examination resumed— I have stated all that Mr. Pym ever told me.

By the Judge— I do not recollect that I said any more before the Coroner. Pym was very intimate with me.

Examined by Mr. Cockburn— I have known Mr. Pym for some time. He bears the very highest character in the regiment, both as an officer, a gentleman, and a man of humanity.

Mrs. Scorsmore, examined by Mr. Smith— My husband keeps a lodging-house in Portsea. Mr. Hawkey lodged with my husband in May last. I remember his coming home in uniform on the 20th. He put plain clothes on and left the house soon after. I never saw him after that time. Mrs. Hawkey was fetched away in the evening about half-past ten. She came back the next day about two, and was there for about a week after.

Examined by Mr. Cockburn— I remember Mr Hawkey asking me on the 19th to do him a favour. He gave me some directions. He asked me if I knew Mr. Seton. I said I did. He said he expected him to call on him, and begged me if he did, to come into the room frequently, and mot leave them together, as Mr. Seton had dreadfully insulted Mrs. Hawkey, as she had told him. Mr. Jenkins, examined by Mr. Rawlinson— I am a surgeon residing at Gosport. I remember being called to attend a gentleman at Stokes Bay, on the evening of the 23d of May last. The person who called me was an officer's servant. I went in a fly. When I got there I found a boat waiting, which took me on board a yacht. I there found Mr. Seton lying on a board. The name of the yacht was the Orion. I knew Seton. He was pale, weak, and exhausted; pulse nearly imperceptible. I gave him brandy and water in small quantities. I asked where the wound was. I was told it was in the hip. It was covered with a ligature. The wound had ceased to bleed. He appeared to have lost a great deal of blood. I believe the bleeding ceased owing to the faintness. On my way to the hotel I sent for Mr. Mortimer. I there with him examined the wound. I found it in the hip. It passed through the *liocastor major*, passed through she volls of the belly, and out at the groin. There was much inflammation. The wound was from a bullet. It entered at the right side. I washed and dressed 

the wound, and stopped with him until ten. I saw him again at twelve. He was then going on well; no further bleeding took place. I continued to attend him until his death. Dr. Stewart joined in attendance.The next day the patient went on pretty favourably until the 27th. He, however, was never out of danger from that wound. On the 27th, I, for the first time, found a tumour (which had existed from the first) pulsating. From that circumstance we thought that one of the principal arteries had been wounded and were borne out in this by the description of the flow of blood taking place a the time the wound was given. It flowed at that time in fits. I have attended many cases of gunshot wounds. I consulted with Dr. Steward. We thought the patient in great danger, that sloughing might take place, and that death would instantly ensue. It was a case of false aneurism which might burst at any time, if sloughing took place. There had been a clot which had been thrown off on the seventh day by suppuration of the slough. The effect of that would be to cause bleeding. We waited twenty-four hours. It did not much increase in size. The pulsation was much the same. We continued to apply ice and cold applications. On the 29th the tumour had increased in size. We considered him in great danger. It pulsated with every stroke of the artery. On the 30th much the same. On the 29th we had informed the patient of his danger. We determined on having further advice from London. We then thought that if there was any increase of pulsation, it would be it necessary to tie the artery above the wound. This was a dangerous operation particularly on this patient. I should not have determined on this if I had not thought it absolutely necessary to save the patient's life. It was the only chance. Mr. Liston arrived from London on the evening of the 30th. Mr. Thomson and Mr. Potter arrived with him. They examined the wound and all concurred in the necessity of the operation. The next morning Mr. Liston performed the operation; it was most skilfully done, scarce two table spoonfuls of blood were lost after that operation. The pulsation ceased in the tumour during the night after. The patient suffered considerably. I remained with him all night. He died on the Monday morning between seven and eight.

Cross examined by Mr. G. Cockburn. [An engraving by Mr. Quain was here shown to the witness of that part of the human body] — I never study from plates: they are of no use to me. I think this may be a correct delineation of the human body. Mr. Quain's plates are of authority in the profession, but are of no use to me. I cannot describe the course of the ball with a pencil. [The witness here described the course of the ball by pointing it out on his own person.] Ihe deceased was very fat. I heard that the blood spirted out in a jet. After I washed the wound there was no further bleeding. Originally the bleeding was stopped by the fainting, nothing else would have saved his life then. The ligature that had been used had not been put on the proper place. The circulation was restored to a certain degree on the next day after the wound was given. In the early stage I had used tepid applications. He had done well, considering the circumstances, up to the time we discovered the pulsation of the tumour. He was in a bad condition of body. I was led to think that a considerable artery was wounded by the way the spouting of the blood had been described. I thought, also, that a main branch of the femoral artery was wounded. I think it possible that even the main femoral artery had been wounded by a ball, as there was a place for the blood to flow out. The patient might not have died immediately if he fainted, but this is very improbable, it is just possible. I thought the artery wounded must be either the epigastric or a branch of the femoral artery. The artery tied by Mr. Liston was the external iliac artery. This is in the pelvis.

At this part of the examination the Learned Judge intimated, that as it was evident, from the cross-examination, what point the Learned Counsel was about to take, he at once said, that where a party had received a dangerous wound, and he *bona fide* availed himself of the surgical treatment within his reach, and that treatment was *bona fide*, that even if the patient died from that treatment, and it was unskilful, still the party giving the person the wound is answerable for giving it.

Mr. Cockburn pressed upon the Learned Judge that he should be able to make it clear that the death was in consequence of an improper operation, and therefore that as the point had never been expressly decided, that where a party died, under these circumstances, the party giving the wound could be held liable for the death, he should reserve the point if necessary, and leave the question to the Jury of whether the operation was necessary, and whether the patient died in consequence of it.

Mr. Justice Erle— I think I should lay down the law to the injury on this question, and not leave it to them. I have formed an opinion on the case, that although the proximate cause of death was the operation, still the party giving the wound is liable for the result. I shall so tell the jury. It may he taken in this case, if you prove that the proximate cause of death was the operation, which operation was considered necessary under all the circumstances of the case, and this may be so taken, and at any stage of the case you may tender evidence of other medical men in contradiction of those called for the prosecution, to show that the operation was not necessary, and that death would not have ensued if it had not been done, you may then argue the point of law arising on these facts, I will give my opinion, and then, if it be necessary, you shall have an opportunity to review my opinion as to the law on these facts, by taking the opinion of the Judges upon it.

This course was assented on both sides.

Dr. John Mortimer examined by Mr. Smith— I am a doctor of medicine, practising at Gosport. I have been a naval surgeon, and was at Haslar Hospital. I was called in to attend the deceased. [The witness then described the wound in nearly the same words as the last witness.] There was no alteration in the appearance of the wound from the first time I saw it until the seventh day. I then observed pulsation in the tumour. It continued and increased; on the 28th more distinct, and still more on the 30th. The moment I discovered the pulsation I told the patient that there was a new feature in the wound which would render farther measures necessary. The appearances were indicative of danger to life. I mentioned this on the 28th. In my judgment farther measures were necessary. I stated to the surgeons that it would be necessary to intercept the communication between the torn vessel and the false aneurism— the tumour. Mr. Liston arrived from London on the Friday. Mr Potter was with him. We had a consultation— the five medical men. The result of the consultation was, that we thought a branch emanating from the femoral artery was wounded; that it was throwing out its blood with increased force into the false aneurism; and that it required a ligature to prevent this flow. The operation was performed by Mr. Liston. The next morning we also consulted as to the mode in which the operation should be performed, and concurred in the mode. Mr. Liston is a very eminent surgeon. The operation was most skilfully performed. The pulsation ceased in a moment after the operation was performed. There was no unfavourable symptom until the Saturday: in the morning there was a slight degree of tenderness and in the evening peritoneal inflammation came on, and increased until Sunday, when the patient died. I do not attribute the peritoneal inflammation to the loss of blood caused by the bullet wound, or disturbance to the system in consequence. In my opinion, the operation was the only chance of saving life. The immediate cause of both was the peritoneal inflammation subsequent to the operation.

Cross-examined by Mr. Kinglake— In my opinion, the patient would have shortly died of haemorrage, if the operation had not been resorted to; probably in twenty-four hours. I never expressed an opinion that he might have lived twelve months. I do not think I have ever said that he might have lived twelve months. Certainly he might by possibility have lived twelve months, but there was no human probability of his so doing.

Dr. Steward, examined by Mr. Rawlinson— I am a surgeon in the navy. In the course of my practice I have seen many gun-shot wounds. I attended the deceased. From the minute I saw him I considered him in danger. From the gun-shot wound he had lost a considerable quantity of blood. That is seldom the case in gun-shot wounds, unless an artery was wounded. He received the very best treatment. He was never out of imminent danger. I always stated that at first, the dangerous period had not come. The dangerous period I considered to be when the coagulum came off, as then the sloughing would take place, when the pulsation came on I was confirmed in mv opinion as to an artery being wounded, nothing but the operation upon the iliac artery, which was so beautifully performed, could have saved his life. I often told the patient the danger he was in. I did so in consequence of his questions, and I thought I ought not to deceive him. I told his friends he ought to make his will, and in consequence he did so, and on the Friday received the sacrament. I was with the deceased until his death. I sat up with him on the Sunday night, and on the Monday. The medical men relieved each other in their attendance on him. He was senseless. On the Sunday he asked me if there was any chance of his recovery. I evaded the question. He took hold of my hand and said, "I am not afraid to die, tell me the worst." I told him there was no hope of his recovery. He wept very much, and said, "I thank you and all your medical friends for the care and very great kindness you have shown to me. He then made a statement to me. [Question—"What was the statement - (by Cockburn).] Often I told him there was no hope. I went away. The statement was made on my return, some hours after; in the interim the other persons were in attendance. After I returned, he asked me if I thought he was better.

By the Judge— I think that the patient was in the immediate apprehension of death.

Mr. Cockburn— The opinion of this witness on that point cannot affect the question of whether he felt that his death was approaching, and was without hope.

Mr. Justice Erle— If the deceased was in immediate apprehension of death, it is enough to make his declarations evidence, although he do not give up hope. 

Mr Cockburn— I contend that if the deceased had the slightest scintilla of hope of recovery, his declarations can not be received. This was ruled by Mr. Justice Cresswell in Bonner's case. Now, here it seems clear that the patient never relinquished the hope of recovery; for, after being told there was no hope, he the next day asked whether he was better.

Mr. Justice Erle— I am of opinion that the declaration is admissible, for I think that when persons speak under the apprehension of death, they speak the truth; for here Dr Stewart tells him that there is no hope, and he thereupon weeps, and thanks the medical men for their attention; and, therefore, although he may not have given up all hope of recovery, yet he was in such a solemn state of mind as to render his statements admissible.

Examination resumed— He said, well knowing my opinion, "I know not why I was shot." He said he went on the ground, and if he had taken the position as proposed by his opponent he would then be in his opponent's state, and his opponent in his state. He said he saw Mr. Hawkey present the first time, and it did not go off. On the second time it did go off and went through his body. He said he himself fired both times. He said, in answer to my question, that he had no doubt but that Mr. Hawkey was the man who fired.

Cross-examined by Mr. Cockburn— My quarrel with Dr. Williams had nothing to do with this matter. I had no conversation as to the cause of his death but this.

Mr. James Allen, examined by Mr. Smith— I am one of the surgeons of Haslar Hospital. I have had great practice in gun-shot wounds. I made a *post-mortem* examination of the body of Mr. Seton. I found, on following the course of the wound, that it had wounded an artery within the external iliac, or the epigastric artery. It was one inch from the back of the femoral artery. In my judgment the operation performed by Mr. Listen was necessary. It is impossible to say that the patient would have died if the operation had not taken place, for I have known some aneurismal tumours cured spontaneously, and aneurism is more likely to be cured than a false one. [Here the witness described the difference between the two.] I do not think any one can say either that a similar aneurism would be cured spontaneously, or whether death would ensue. I have known aneurism cured spontaneously where a co-agulation had formed over the wound in the artery, and the vessel had become obliterated.

Re-examined— In my judgment there was very little probability of the artery being healed spontaneously.

Mr. Cockburn proposed to ask "Whether another and better operation might not have been performed?"

To this it was objected, on behalf of the prosecution, that there was no question before the Jury that the medical man had not used proper skill.

Mr. Justice Erle said that he should not leave that question to the Jury, or allow evidence to be given to show that there were other and better modes of treatment of the wound, but would reserve the point if it were necessary.

Mr. Robert Liston examined by Mr. Robinson— I am a surgeon, practising in London. I came down on the morning of the 30th to see the deceased. I saw him, and held a consultation with the other surgeons. I examined the wound. The patient had every symptom of having lost a great deal of blood, which I was told had taken place at the time of the wound. Some branch of an artery must have been wounded. The danger was of the bruised parts sloughing off. There must have been a coagulation, or on that coming off blood was effused, and a false annexation was formed. The only practical operation, under these circumstances, was to tie the iliac artery. [Question— "Do you think this was the proper mode of treatment," objected to by Mr. Cockburn, on the ground the Learned Judge had just ruled — that he was not to call witness in contradiction. Not pressed.] In my opinion there was no other operation which could have been resorted to which would have been useful.

Mr. Samson, examined by Mr. Smith— I came down with Mr. Liston. I concurred in his treatment of the case by the operation.

Mr. Potter— I am a surgeon, practising in London. I also came down with Mr. Listen, examined the wound, and concur in the treatment. I am one of the demonstrators at the University Hospital.

This concluded the case for the prosecution.

Mr. Cockburn submitted there was no case to go to the jury. The indictment stated that the cause of the death of Captain Seton was by a certain wound caused by a leaden bullet, shot from a certain pistol. Now, as the death was not immediately caused by the bullet, but by an operation performed in consequence of it, it should have been so stated on the face of the indictment. The death was not caused immediately by the bullet, but by putting in the surgeon's knife.

Mr. Sergeant Kinglake, was heard on the same side, and contended that the truth of the means of killing must be correctly set out. He also cited Hickman's case (Roscoe's Criminal Evidence, 651; Evercose, ibid, 645). 

Mr. Justice Erle, without hearing the other side, said that it appeared to him that when once a wound had been given the medical treatment was one of the consequences of it, and need not be stated, but was to be calculated as a portion of the disease; if, however, on the point of law already reserved, or in any other way you can raise a doubt, your client shall have the benefit of it.

The Court then adjourned at six o'clock for a quarter of an hour, and had not met when our report left.

Morning Post - Monday 09 March 1846

WESTERN CIRCUIT.— Winchester, March 6.

Crown Court.

(Before Mr. Justice Erle.)

The Portsea Duel.

In our last report of this case we were enabled to carry down the proceedings to the objection taken by Mr. Cockburn to the indictment; after which the Court adjourned for a short time. On the return of the jury,

Mr. Cockburn addressed them for the defence. He felt confident that the jury would watch this case with the greatest jealousy, and that they would not, if they could by any possibility avoid it, return a verdict which would class the gentleman who was now upon his trial with the midnight assassin and the clandestine poisoner. It was true that, according to the strict letter of the law, the man who in a fair and honourable contest shot his antagonist in a duel, or he who on such an occasion stood by his friend as a second, was considered guilty of murder, and he would not disguise from himself the fact that the Learned Judge whe presided over the trial, and sat far removed above the feelings and passions of mankind, would tell them that this was the case; but it was the law to select as the judges of the fact men chosen from the body of the people as the representatives of their sentiments and their feelings, and it was their privilege, in a case like the present, to stand between the strict iron rigour of the law and the prisoner, and to preserve from a fall of ignominy and disgrace, the man whose high sense of honour and exalted sentiments of friendship have caused him to transgress the letter of the law, that he might name the most exalted authorities, both living and dead, statesmen, warriors, even those who had sat to administer justice, who had been involved in transactions of this kind, and whose character and reputation had 'never been there tainted. He might name Pitt and Fox, and Erskine, and a host of the living and the dead, to corroborate this assertion, but he would refrain from doing so, and would at once address himself to the particular facts of this case, confident that they would never find as a murderer, a young gentleman, whose conduct had been directed by th» most chivalrous feeling of friendship and honor. The Learned Counsel then stated that he would call witnesses to prove that Mrs. Hawkey had been most grossly insulted by Mr. Seton, who had even presumed to tempt her by an offer of pecuniary recompense to forget the duty which she owed to herself, her husband, and society. Could they wonder after this, that Mr. Hawkey was excited, and used violent language, and when Mr. Seton added to the insult and the injury, by himself sending the challenge, could they wonder that Mr. Hawkey accepted it, and that Mr. Pym, whom he had ever treated as a brother, had consented to stand by him as a friend on that occasion. His honour, his reputation as an officer and a gentleman, peremptorily required this; and he firmly believed, that if any of the Jury had been called upon under similar circumstances, the chivalrous spirit of Englishmen which beats within their bosoms would have induced them to do the same, although his friend, Mr. Rawlinson, might have pointed to the strict letter of the law, and told them that they were guilty. The Learned Counsel then commented at great length on the evidence which had been produced on the part of the prosecution, and contended that the facts proved were not legally sufficient to show that the prisoner had been present at the time the shot was fired, which it was said was the cause of the death of the unfortunate Mr. Seton. He then proposed to call Mrs. Hawkey, but the Learned Judge ruled that her evidence was not admissible. He then concluded a most powerful and eloquent speech of three hours' duration, and called a host of witnesses, officers of the corps to which the prisoner had belonged, who gave the highest testimony to his character, as a gentleman, an officer, and a man of most humane, quiet, peaceable disposition.

The learned Judge then most carefully summed up the case to the jury, who after a few minutes' deliberation, returned a verdict of *Not Guilty*.

This verdict was received in a most crowded court with a burst of applause, which was with difficulty repressed, and Mr. Pym left the court surrounded with his friends.

THE FATAL DUEL AT GOSPORT.

(From our Reporter.)

TRIAL OF LIEUTENANT HAWKEY, R.M.

(Before Sir T. J. Platt, Knight.)

WINCHESTER, Thursday, July 16.

This morning, at an early hour, similar preparations were made in the Court for this trial as on the trial of Lieutenant Pym, at the last Spring Assizes, extra accommodation being made for the gentlemen of the press. The hall and every staircase and approach was densely crowded by well-dressed parties, anxious to gain admission, the Grand Jury benches and other parts being principally occupied by fashionably-dressed ladies. At nine o'clock the Learned Judge arrived. Several trials of minor interest had first to be disposed of. At about a quarter to ten Captain Henry Morehead Hawkey was placed at the bar. His firm demeanour, though becoming the awful situation he was placed in, created a deep sympathy, particularly among the fair sex. He, with a firm voice, pleaded "Not guilty," to the charge of maliciously shooting, with a leaden bullet, Lieutenant Alexander Seaton, and the like on the Coroner's inquisition.

Counsel for the prosecution, Mr. Rawlinson and Mr. Montague Smith.

Counsel for the defence, Mr. Colburn, Q.C, and Mr. Sergeant Kingslake.

Mr. Rawlinson opened the case. He said that though much time has elapsed since this unfortunate affair, various reports are still afloat, upon which but little reliance ought to be placed, and I trust none will have any influence on the minds of the jury— a fair and impartial decision is all that is looked for or required from them. The prisoner is charged with having shot and killed at Blackdown one Lieutenant Seaton, as the law stands no person is justified in taking away the blood of his fellow-creatures in a private quarrel. THE FATAL DUEL AT GOSPORT.

The following sketch of the conclusion of the trial of Lieutenant Hawkey, R.M., appeared in all but a small part of our impression yesterday:—

Mr. Cockburn having concluded a mast eloquent address, called Colonel Swaile, Colonel Gordon, Colonel Stephens, and a great many officers of the marine corps and other gentlemen, who gave the prisoner the highest character as an officer and a gentleman, and as a person of a very peaceable and kind disposition.

Mr Baron Platt then proceeded to sum up the case to the Jury, and laid the law down to them to be, that if two persons go out to fight a duel after a previous quarrel, and one kill the other, all persons, whether principals or seconds, are guilty of murder. If, therefore, they thought that the evidence in this case made out to their satisfaction that Mr. Hawkey had shot Mr. Seton in a duel, it would be their bounden duty to find him guilty on the present indictment. He then went minutely through the evidence, commenting upon it as he went on: and concluded by telling them, that whatever they might think the provocation had been to the prisoner by the conduct of Mr. Seton to his (the prisoner's) wife, and however they might commiserate the wounded feelings of the husband, yet they must not allow that feeling to prevent them doing their duty, and if therefore, they thought the charge was made out in evidence against the prisoner, they were bound to convict him.

At the conclusion of the learned baron's address, the jury without the hesitation of a single instant— almost without the formality of consulting together—returned a verdict of *Not Guilty*.

The announcement was received with a violent burst of approbation, which the officers of the Court strove in vain to repress. TRIAL OF LIEUTENANT HAWKEY FOR THE WILFUL MURDER OF LIEUTENANT SETON IN A DUEL.

*Henry Charles Morehead Hawkey* surrendered to take his trial for the wilful murder of James Alexander Seton. The court was crowded, and the trial seemed to cause the greatest interest.

The indictment was read, and it charged the prisoner with the wilful murder of James Alexander Seton, by it shooting at him with a pistol, loaded with a leaden bullet, and giving him a mortal wound on the right side. He was also charged with a like offence by the coroner's inquisition.

The prisoner who is about twenty-six year's of age, pleaded Not Guilty in a firm voice. He appeared to have suffered a good deal of anxiety, and looked very pale and care-worn.

Mr. Rawlinson and Mr. Montague Smith appeared for the Crown; Mr. Cockburn, Q.C., and Mr. Sergeant Kinglake, defended the prisoner.

Mr. Rawlinson, in opening the case for the prosecution, exhorted the jury to banish from their minds all reports which they might have heard respecting the case, and to give their verdict upon the evidence alone. The prisoner at the bar stood charged with having killed Lieutenant Seton in a duel which took place on Brown Down, near Gosport. The law, as the jury would doubtless hear it expounded by the learned judge, was that if a man went out deliberately to seek the blood of his fellow-man in a private quarrel, and killed him, he was guilty of the crime of murder. The learned counsel then proceeded to give a short outline of the facts of the case, which as they have been already fully reported more than once, it is unnecessary to repeat. We will merely recall to the recollection of our readers that Lieutenant Hawkey was stationed at Portsmouth, where he resided in lodgings with his wife. They became acquainted with Mr. and Mrs. Seton, who lived at Southsea. On the 10th of May, last year, there was a ball at Southsea, and all the parties were there, as well as Lieut. Pym. During the ball some altercation arose between the prisoner and Mr. Seton, and they retired into a card room where they remained ten minutes or a quarter of an hour.— Next morning, at eight o'clock, a gentleman called on the prisoner, and left his card, and it would appear that that gentleman was sent by Mr. Seton with a challenge to Mr. Hawkey. This was the first indication of danger he discovered. The pulsation continued to increase, and on the 29th the symptoms rendered further steps necessary. Witness then had a consultation with Mr. Jenkins and Dr. Stewart every day. The state of the tumour certainly rendered an operation necessary. The operation was tying the iliac artery. In witness's opinion no other operation offered the same chances of success. Mr. Seton was a little exhausted after the operation, but the operation was perfectly successful, and the pulsation entirely ceased. Mr. Seton died on the Monday. Witness was present at the *post mortem* examination, and saw that the course of the wound was superficial on the muscles of the belly, and then divided a branch of the iliac artery. In witness's judgment peritoneal inflamation was the immediate cause of death. The gun-shot wound was a dangerous one.

If it had, it would been death in a different form— from hemorhage, and not from peritoneal inflamation.

Dr Stewart surgeon, was examined— From the first moment he saw Mr. Seton, he considered him in imminent danger, and communicated that fact to his friends. The witness also described the nature and course of the wound, and generally confirmed the preceding medical testimony. On the Sunday morning after the operation, Mr. Seton was much worse, and witness communicated his danger to him. The witness proceeded to say, Mr. Seton repeatedly inquired my opinion, but I evaded the question as much as I could; but he said, "I am not afraid to die." This was towards Monday morning. He said, "I am most thankful to you and the medical men who have attended me. I am aware of your opinion. Do you think there is the least chance?" I told him there was not the least hope— none in the world. He repeated to me a conversation he had had with me during the night. He said, "I repeat to you, as I did before, I know not why I am shot." He wept bitterly. He rad previously received the sacrament. He had seen his lawyer and made his will on the Saturday morning previous to the operation. He appeared to believe what I said, that there was no hope for him. This was between six and seven o'clock on Monday morning.

By Mr Cockburn— I sat up with him all night. It was during the night that he asked me if there was any hope. It was on the second time he asked me that I told him there was no hope. He did not afterwards repeat the question. He died about seven o'clock on the Monday evening. I was with him repeatedly in the course of Monday. By Mr. Cockburn— The artery taken up was the external iliac artery.

By the Judge— By tying that artery the circulation was cut off from this tumour. In my opinion the proper artery was taken up.

This closed the case for the prosecution.

Mr. Cockburn then submitted that upon the evidence in this case the offence of murder, according to the English law, was not made out. It appeared that a wound was inflicted, in consequence of which an operation was thought necessary; and that, from the results of that operation, the person wounded died. No decisions or any distinct authorities were to be found in which this had been held to be murder. The question is alluded to in 1 Russ. on Crimes, 505, but the cases there cited were instances of the death being occasioned by the physical consequences of the wound, though somewhat remote. In the Scotch law cases had occurred analagous to the present, and there it was held not to be murder. He cited Alison's Principles of Criminal Law in Scotland, p. 145.

Mr. Cockburn then objected that the cause of death was not rightly described, as being by "the wound" by a pistol bullet. 
 
His Lordship also expressed his opinion to be against the objection, but he said that, as Mr. Justice Erle had on a former occasion reserved the point, he should do the same.

Mr. Cockburn then rose and addressed the jury on behalf of the prisoner, in a most eloquent and stirring speech, which lasted two hours, and of which we can give only a brief outline. He recapitulated the circumstances, already familiar to the public, which occurred prior to the fatal encounter, and dwelt with much earnestness and energy upon the deep provocation which Lieut. Hawkey had received in the base attempt upon the chastity of his wife made by Mr. Seton. He commented with great force upon the evidence for the prosecution, contending that as it stood before the jury it demonstrated both the provocation the prisoner had received, and the absence of vindictivenss on his part. With respect to the long absence of Lieut. Hawkey from his regiment since the fatal occurrence, it had not been the result of his own free will, but under the constraint as it were of those who conducted his defence. A strong popular feeling had been excited at the time of the fatal occurrence, and it had been thought right to allow time for that feeling to subside before the prisoner should be put upon his trial; and, moreover, it was only an act of justice to Mr. Pym, the old friend of Mr. Hawkey, that his trial should take place under the most favourable circumstance, and if Mr. Pym and Mr. Hawkey had been tried at the same time, Mrs. Hawkey being the wife of one of the prisoners, Mr. Pym could not have had the benefit of her evidence. That was the reason the trial of Mr. Hawkey had been deferred, but he had this day surrendered to take his trial of his own free will and unrestrained choice. Mr. Hawkey, however, was not tried under circumstances so advantageous as Mr. Pym, for by the law he could not have the benefit of the evidence of his wife, who could have detailed all the circumstances of provocation. The learned in counsel, in commenting upon the evidence, represented the great danger that would arise if juries were to allow detached fragments of conversation, which had been overheard, to bias their minds against a prisoner on trial for his life. He showed that no vindictiveness could be inferred from the practice in the shooting gallery, inasmuch as there had been, in fact, no practice at all, only three shots having been fired, just sufficient to try the pistols. The evidence also showed that both Pym and Hawkey were ignoranit of the practice of duelling; for the former was obliged even to observe how the pistol was loaded by the man in attendance in the gallery. He called the attention of the jury to the facts, that Seton was the challenger, after the deadly injury he had attempted to inflict upon the honour and peace of the prisoner, that at the meeting Hawkey's pistol missed fire once, that in that position it was impossible for him to propose an accommodation, while it was quite in Seton's power, as the challenger, to have done so— but he persisted, and he consequently fired twice, while Mr. Hawkey had only fired once. The learned counsel then made some general observations on duelling, and the law relating to it, the substance of which has already appeared in the report of his speech on the trial of Mr. Pym, and urged upon the jury not to inflict the last peualties of the lawv upon a man who had met his antagonist in fair and equal combat, and class him in the same catagory with the secret and cowardly assassin. He challenged the production of a case where a jury had convicted of wilful murder a man who had met his adversary in fair fight. He then contended that no reliance was to be placed upon the last statement of Mr. Seton, and beyond that there was no strict legal evidence, such as the jury had a right to demand in such a case, that the hand of Mr. Hawkey caused the death of Mr. Seton. The learned counsel then argued that the cause of death was not properly set forth in the indictment, inasmuch as it was described as accruing from a leaden bullet, whereas it was proved by the medical witnesses to have accrued from peritoneal inflamation, resulting from the surgical operation.

Mr. Cockburn having concluded his most eloquent address, called Colonel Swale, Colonel Gordon, Colonel Stephens, and a great many officers of the Marine corps and other gentlemen, who gave the prisoner the highest character as an officer and a gentlenman, and as a person of a very peaceable and kind disposition.

Mr. Baron Platt then proceeded to sum up the case to the jury, and laid the law down to them to be, that if two persons go out to fight a duel after previous quarrel, and one kill the other, all persons, whether principals or seconds, are guilty of murder. If, therefore, they thought that the evidence in this case made out to their satisfaction that Hawkey had shot Mr. Seton in a duel, it would be their bounden duty to find him guilty on the present indictment. He then went minutely through the evidence, commenting upon it as he went on; and concluded by telling them, that whatever they might think the provocation had been to the prisoner by the conduct of Mr. Seton to his (the prisoner's) wife, and however they might commiserate the wounded feelings of the husband, yet they must not allow that feeling to prevent them doing their duty; and if therefore, they thought the charge was made out in evidence against the prisoner, they were bound to convict him.

At the conclusion of the learned baron's address the jury, without the hesitation of a single instant — almost it might be seen, without the formality of consulting together — returned a verdict of Not Guilty.
  
The announcement was received with a vehemet burst of approbation, which the officers of the court strove in vain to repress. The learned judge commanded silence, and was compelled to order one of the most prominent and noisy of the crowd into custody. The culprit, however, managed to abscond; and, in truth, nobody seemed disposed to take very violent efforts for his apprehension.
  
Lieut. Hawkey, immediately on his liberation, was surrounded by friends and brother officers, offering him their hearty congratulations; and upon his exit from the court he was loudly cheered by the people.

Mr. Baron Platt was also saluted with hearty huzzas as his carriage passed through the streets of the fine old city of Winchester.
